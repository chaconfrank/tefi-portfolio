The project involved rethinking a very outdated website and turning it into a responsive experience that would guide the user from discovering an activity through to requesting and managing their booking.

When I joined the project, the HandiSport website worked mainly as an informational repository. The activities section was especially long: each offering appeared one after another, with large blocks of text, little visual hierarchy, and no tools to help find an activity based on each person's needs.

Booking wasn't part of an obvious flow either. The CTA appeared at the bottom of the page and it wasn't clear that it was a booking action; from there, the journey ended up routing to a generic contact form. Other areas of the site followed a similar structure, with a lot of empty space, information that was hard to scan, and little consistency between sections.

</CaseText>

<Diagram
  imagen={homeContactoAntiguos}

<CaseText compacta conclusion columna="izquierda">

The challenge wasn't just updating the look. An informational website had to become an understandable journey, from "I want to find an activity" to "I want to request my spot".

We didn't need to create a new identity. HandiSport already had a recognizable brand built around orange; the work consisted of carrying that identity into a more consistent, easier-to-maintain interface.

I worked from a Material Design base and a Flutter theme to define a tonal palette, color roles, and a shared typographic hierarchy. Montserrat was used mainly for headlines and higher-hierarchy elements, while Roboto was applied to content, labels, and functional text.

/>

<CaseText compacta>

I didn't build a full Design System. The goal was to establish enough shared rules so that forms, filters, cards, states, galleries, and management tools would feel like part of the same product.

<CaseSection
  compacta
  id="encontrar-actividades"
  numero="03"
  etiqueta="Finding activities based on each need"
  titulo="Turning accessibility into part of discovery"
  composicion="apilada"
>

One of my main UX contributions was proposing that accessibility needs shouldn't appear only as information within an activity's page, but could also be used to find compatible activities.

Together with Samuel, we defined the categories the platform would include:

<p class="font-medium text-ink">Hearing access · Cognitive support · Visual access · Mobility access · Sensory access.</p>

From the Profile, the user can add the needs that apply to them or indicate that they have no disability. Based on that information, I designed the auto-filter option, meant to reduce the effort of reselecting the same filters every time the catalog is checked.

<Fragment slot="media">

<Diagram
  imagen={perfilAutofiltrado}
  desplazable={false}
  alt="The Profile before and after. Before: no needs configured, with the «Añadir discapacidades» (Add disabilities) field empty and auto-filter off. After: disabilities added (Hearing Access, Cognitive Support, Visual Access, Mobility Access, Sensory Access) and auto-filter on, with the «¿Qué es el auto filtrado?» (What is auto-filtering?) link."
/>

<CaseText compacta columna="izquierda">

Auto-filtering came with an accessible explanation right inside the interface. The goal was for the user to understand that the platform would use the needs saved in their Profile to show them activities adapted to those needs, and to be able to go straight to configuring them if they hadn't done so yet.

In the Activities section, I also added a small journey guide to reduce doubts:

<p class="font-medium text-ink">Auto-filter <span aria-hidden="true" class="text-amaia-ink">→</span><span class="sr-only">,</span> Booking <span aria-hidden="true" class="text-amaia-ink">→</span><span class="sr-only">,</span> Request.</p>

</CaseText>

<Diagram
  imagen={actividadesFiltros}

/>

<CaseText compacta columna="izquierda">

The same logic continues inside each activity. The chips show again which needs it's adapted to before the user reaches the “Reservar mi plaza” (Book my place) CTA. That way, the activity's compatibility stays visible throughout the journey and doesn't depend on remembering the filter applied earlier.

</CaseText>

Accessibility stopped being just a feature of the activity page and became part of how content is discovered.

<CaseSection
  compacta
  id="reserva-y-gestion"
  numero="04"
  etiqueta="From booking to management"
  titulo="Designing the journey after finding the activity"
  destacado="Needs don't belong only to the booking: they belong to each person taking part in it."
>

Requesting a spot didn't always mean booking for a single person. The form also had to adapt to groups and allow each participant's needs to be registered independently.

As the number of attendees increases, per-person blocks appear. Each one allows indicating who's participating and what accessibility needs they have. Accordions help keep the form manageable even when the booking includes several people.

<Diagram

Group booking: with 5 people, accordions appear from Person 1 to Person 5; Person 1 «soy yo» (that's me) with their disabilities added, Person 2 with their own disability selector, and the rest collapsed. Callout: Needs per participant."
/>

<CaseText compacta>

Once the request is submitted, the user can track its progress from My bookings. I designed a distinct visual hierarchy for states like Pending, Confirmed, and Expired, plus filters to access other states like Rejected or Cancelled.

The same request has a complementary view for the HandiSport team. From internal management, a pending request shows the information needed to review it and allows accepting or rejecting it.

</CaseText>

<CaseSection

The experience didn't end on the public side. HandiSport needed tools to maintain activities, requests, and content from within the platform itself.

When creating an activity, the administrator can define a title, subtitle, description, content, compatible accessibility needs, notes, and gallery. Associating those needs at the moment the activity is created is what later allows them to appear as chips, filters, and auto-filtering criteria.

Callouts: Activity management and Accessibility needs."

<CaseText compacta columna="izquierda">

I also designed the article creation flow so the team could maintain editorial content from the platform. The form allows preparing a title, subtitle, short description, content, and gallery, as well as selecting a cover image and describing the pictures.

</CaseText>

<CaseText compacta columna="izquierda">

So publishing wouldn't depend on always having the same amount of visual material, I designed four layout variants based on the number of available photographs:

<p class="font-medium text-ink">3 photos · 4 photos · 5 photos · +5 photos.</p>

Each variant reorganizes the content and keeps its mobile adaptation, so the team can publish different pieces without breaking the site's overall consistency.

</CaseText>

/>

<CaseText compacta conclusion columna="izquierda">

The product had to be clear for the person booking, but also easy to maintain for whoever publishes and manages the content.

</CaseText>

  numero="06"
  etiqueta="Outcome + scope"
  titulo="An experience connecting users and internal management"
>

The final design expanded the old website's scope to cover much of the full journey: discovering activities, checking their accessibility needs, requesting spots, checking statuses, managing bookings, and maintaining activities and articles from within the platform itself.

The work was approached responsively for desktop and mobile and was developed in close collaboration with the technical team. Samuel centralized communication with HandiSport, and I worked especially closely with Daniela, on the frontend, sharing designs, visual assets, photographs, copy, and the criteria needed to turn the screens into product.

My involvement ended with the handoff of the designs. I didn't conduct interviews or tests with real users, and I don't have any later metrics that would let me attribute quantitative results to the redesign.
